If you own an index fund or an iPhone — and most people own at least one — this one touches you twice. Apple just reported its **best fiscal year ever**, beat Wall Street's estimates, and the stock *fell* anyway.

The strange part: the number that spooked investors isn't the scary one. It's actually the cushioned one — and the real squeeze lands later. Here's the plain-English version.

## Best year ever — and the stock fell

For its fiscal fourth quarter (the July–September period), Apple reported:

- **Revenue of $114.5 billion**, up 12% year over year, beating the roughly $113.4 billion expected.
- **Earnings of $2.10 per share**, up 14%, ahead of estimates (and including a $0.05 benefit from a tariff refund).

Zoom out and the full fiscal year was a record: **$478.8 billion** in revenue and earnings of **$8.98 per share**, up 20% — the best fiscal year in the company's history. iPhone revenue was $57.6 billion (up about 17%), Mac $10.79 billion (up about 24%), and Services set a record at $31.57 billion.

And yet the stock slid about 3% in after-hours trading on November 2 and roughly 4% the next morning — on a stock that trades near 35 times forward earnings. So what did investors dislike about a record year?

## The one line that did it

Not the results — the *guidance*. Apple told investors to expect a gross margin of just **45–46%** for the December quarter, below the roughly 46.3% Wall Street was modeling. That's a step down from the 47.9% it just reported (which was itself down from 50.1% the quarter before).

The cause, in the company's own words? Apple's CFO, Kevan Parekh, said the guide reflects "primarily the impact of higher memory costs, as well as some costs related to the ramp of Siri AI." (One important label: that 45–46% is a *forecast* for the holiday quarter Apple hasn't reported yet — not the quarter it just closed.)

## Why memory, and why now

Here the cause sits upstream of Apple, so it's worth attributing rather than stating flatly. The memory crunch is widely tied to the AI data-center boom: enormous demand for high-bandwidth and server memory is diverting supply away from the chips that go in phones and laptops. J.P. Morgan estimates DRAM prices have risen more than 400% from early 2024 to the end of 2026, and SK Hynix is reported to be essentially sold out for all of 2026.

And here's the quiet irony: the same squeeze hurting Apple is a windfall for the memory makers it buys from. Companies like Micron, SanDisk and SK Hynix hold the pricing power in a shortage like this. In the hours after Apple's report, some of those names actually ticked higher while Apple fell — a small move, and one that can flip intraday, but a telling one. The AI supply chain is quietly transferring profit from the device makers to the memory suppliers.

## The twist most coverage skips

Now the part that reframes the whole story. That 45–46% guide everyone read as "the pain"? It's the **cushioned** number — not the bottom.

Ahead of the crunch, Apple front-loaded a massive stockpile of memory bought at older, lower prices. Its inventory jumped about **154%** year over year to $14.54 billion, and its manufacturing purchase obligations rose by $33 billion in a single quarter. That cheap carry-in stock is the main reason the holiday quarter's margin isn't *worse*.

Apple's new CEO, John Ternus — on his first earnings call since succeeding Tim Cook on September 1 — said the quiet part out loud. He called memory pricing "unprecedented," said prices are "continuing to increase" beyond December, and acknowledged the company will "see a decreasing benefit from the carry-in inventory on memory over time."

> Translation: the cheap-memory cushion thins out over the coming year. So the real margin squeeze — and the real wave of consumer price increases — most likely lands in 2027, after the stockpile runs down. The December guide isn't the pain. It's the discount.

Apple has already "reluctantly" raised prices on the Mac and iPad (in June) and the iPhone (in September) to offset component costs — and dodged a question about whether more increases are coming.

## What this means for you

Two things. First, if you hold almost any broad index fund, you own Apple — it's one of the most widely held stocks there is, so its margin story quietly rides along in your retirement account. Second, and more practically: if a new phone, laptop, or a memory upgrade is on your list, the cheap window may be closing. That's not a nudge to rush out and buy — it's a heads-up on which way prices are pointing.

## What to watch

- **Apple's December (holiday) quarter**, expected in late January or early February 2027 — the first to actually show that 45–46% margin.
- **Further Apple price increases** across 2027 as the cheap-memory cushion thins.
- **Memory-maker guidance** from SK Hynix, Micron and Samsung on whether the shortage is easing or getting worse.

## The bottom line

Apple just had its best fiscal year ever and still got punished — all because of one forward-looking margin line. But that line is cushioned by a stockpile of cheap memory that won't last, and the company's own CEO says the pressure keeps building. The headline number isn't the pain; it's the discount before it. Worth watching calmly — not worth panicking over.
