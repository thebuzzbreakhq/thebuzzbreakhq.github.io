You probably can't buy this one. Anthropic is still private, so there's no ticker in your brokerage app to tap. And yet it may be the most important market story of the month — because it's shaping up to be one of the largest IPOs ever attempted, and because the number everyone is reacting to is almost certainly the wrong one to watch.

That number is a loss: **$80.7 billion** in the first half of 2026. It reads like a company setting money on fire. But here's what almost no coverage connects: that terrifying loss and the *other* big headline — that investors are pushing to slash the company's valuation — are the **same accounting lever**, pointed in opposite directions. Pull it one way and the loss balloons. Pull it the other, and the loss shrinks. Same company, different number.

Let's walk through it in plain English.

## What's actually happening

Anthropic has filed to go public on the Nasdaq under the proposed ticker **ANT**, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley leading a group of 22 banks. The plan is to raise up to about **$100 billion** and list before US Thanksgiving on November 26 — a size that, if it holds, could make it the largest IPO on record.

The sticking point is price. As recently as early October, the company was being talked about at a valuation as high as **~$2 trillion**. By late October that had eased to about **$1.5 trillion**. Then the mood turned. On November 5, the Nasdaq 100 fell 6.4% and shed about $2.3 trillion in value — what Bloomberg described as its largest single-day decline on record — and prospective investors got cold feet.

Here the reporting splits, and it's worth keeping the sources apart:

- The **Wall Street Journal** reported (Nov 5) that three prospective investors think the ~$1.5 trillion target has to come down, with one putting it closer to **$1 trillion**. A smaller raise of around $80 billion, or an outright delay, were floated — but those came from a single source.
- **Bloomberg** reported (Nov 6) that valuation talks had slid to about **$1.2–1.3 trillion**.

Crucially, there is no official new number. Anthropic has not announced a valuation cut, a price range, or a delay, and it declined to comment. This is a still-developing story — treat any single confident "new valuation" with suspicion, because one doesn't exist yet.

## The $80.7 billion headline

Now the scary part — and why it's less scary than it looks.

The filing showed a first-half 2026 net loss of **$80.7 billion**, up from roughly $13 billion a year earlier. Enormous. But about **$72.4 billion** of that is a *non-cash* accounting charge — money that never left the bank. It comes from the rising fair value of things like convertible notes, SAFEs and derivative liabilities, and the filing attributes it to the rise in Anthropic's *own* valuation.

Read that again, because it's the whole trick: as the company became worth more on paper, the instruments tied to its value got re-marked higher, and the accounting then books that increase as a loss. In other words, a big chunk of the "loss" is a side effect of the valuation going *up*.

## The numbers below the fold

Set that paper charge aside and the picture is very different — though still not rosy, and this is where it's easy to overreach:

- The **operating loss** was only about **$693 million** — not a multi-billion-dollar cash bonfire.
- First-half revenue was about **$16.3 billion**, up from roughly $1.2 billion a year earlier.
- Operating **cash flow was positive $3.9 billion**.

Two honest caveats. First, "cash-flow positive" is not the same as "profitable" — even excluding the paper charge, the first half was still a net loss (on the order of $8 billion), and that positive $3.9 billion is a separate cash-flow measure, not a profit. Second, the obligations are real: the amended filing disclosed about **$895.4 billion** in purchase and lease commitments, substantially all of it compute and infrastructure — much of it struck with companies that are also Anthropic's own backers, a now-familiar bit of circular AI financing — against **$146.6 billion** of cash as of September 30.

So the real questions aren't "how big was the loss?" They're "how fast is revenue growing, and can it ever cover those commitments?"

## The twist most coverage skips

Here's the part that ties the two headlines together. Because that ~$72.4 billion charge *grew as the valuation rose*, the same mechanic runs in reverse. If investors now force the valuation down — from ~$1.5 trillion toward ~$1 trillion — that fair-value charge should mechanically shrink, and could even flip to a paper gain.

> The valuation cut everyone is reporting as bad news could, by itself, make Anthropic's next filing show a dramatically smaller loss — with nothing about the actual business having changed. This is directional accounting, not a forecast.

That's the blind spot: a lot of coverage is treating the "$80.7 billion loss" and the "valuation getting slashed" as two separate disasters, when they're partly two ends of one lever.

## So — healthy company, or not?

Honestly, you can't tell from the headline loss alone, and that's the real lesson here. A terrifying top-line number can be mostly an accounting artifact, while the figures that actually decide whether a company is sound — cash flow, revenue growth, and future obligations — sit below the fold.

You can't put Anthropic in your portfolio. But it's worth watching anyway, because it's a live, real-time gauge of whether the broader AI trade is overheating — and those AI-heavy names *are* concentrated inside the index funds and 401(k)s almost everyone owns.

## What to watch

- Executives and advisers were reported to be meeting around November 6–7 on price, size and timing.
- An updated filing with an actual **price range and share count** — both still blank.
- The **October revenue line**, also left blank, which investors specifically want to see before committing.
- Whether it actually **lists before Thanksgiving (Nov 26)**. Any slip past that window would signal a real delay.

As of this writing, none of those had been settled.

## The bottom line

The $80.7 billion loss is largely a paper charge that grew because the valuation rose. Operations ran cash-flow positive — which is not the same as profitable. The "new" valuation is genuinely unsettled, with different outlets reporting different figures. And the loss and the valuation cut are, in part, the same accounting lever pointed opposite ways. Read past the headline — and remember this is still developing.
